1.  START WITH GOALS AND TIME HORIZON An investment decision should
    begin with a clear goal, a realistic time horizon, and an
    understanding of how much loss the investor can financially and
    emotionally tolerate. Goals may include a short-term planned
    expense, education, a home purchase, or retirement. Money needed
    soon generally should not be exposed to large market fluctuations
    without understanding the risk. A longer time horizon may allow an
    investor to tolerate some volatility, but it does not eliminate risk
    or guarantee a positive return. An emergency reserve and management
    of high-cost debt are important parts of financial planning before
    taking investment risk.

2.  RISK AND RETURN Investment returns are uncertain. A higher expected
    return usually comes with higher or different risks; it is not a
    promise of higher actual performance. Risks may include market-price
    changes, credit/default risk, interest-rate risk, liquidity risk,
    inflation risk, concentration risk, and fraud. Past performance does
    not guarantee future results. Users should understand the product,
    its costs, how returns are generated, the conditions under which
    money can be withdrawn, and what could cause a loss. Do not describe
    any market-linked product as risk-free or guaranteed unless an
    official product document legally supports that exact claim.

3.  DIVERSIFICATION Diversification means spreading exposure across
    different investments, issuers, industries, or asset classes rather
    than relying on one holding. It can reduce the impact of a poor
    result in one part of a portfolio, but it cannot eliminate all risk.
    Investments that appear different may still be exposed to the same
    underlying market factors. A diversified portfolio should be
    considered in the context of the investor’s goals, time horizon,
    liquidity needs, and ability to bear loss. FinPilot should explain
    the concept without prescribing a specific allocation for an
    individual.

4.  MUTUAL FUNDS A mutual fund pools money from investors and invests it
    according to the scheme’s stated objective. Schemes may invest
    mainly in equities, debt securities, a mix of asset classes, or
    other permitted instruments. Different categories carry different
    risks. Equity funds are exposed to share-market movements; debt
    funds can face interest-rate, credit, and liquidity risks; hybrid
    funds combine exposures; and index funds aim to track a specified
    index but still experience market losses and tracking differences.
    Read the scheme information document, key information memorandum,
    factsheet, and current disclosures before investing. Check the
    fund’s objective, benchmark, risk level, expenses, portfolio,
    liquidity conditions, and relevant tax treatment.

5.  SYSTEMATIC INVESTMENT PLANS A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) is a
    method of investing a fixed amount at regular intervals in a mutual
    fund scheme. It can help build a regular investing habit and reduce
    the pressure to choose a single entry date. However, an SIP does not
    protect the investor from market losses, guarantee returns, or
    ensure that the chosen fund is appropriate. Returns depend on the
    underlying scheme and market conditions. Users should understand the
    scheme and review official disclosures rather than assuming that
    regular investing always produces a profit.

6.  EXCHANGE-TRADED FUNDS AND SHARES Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) trade
    on a stock exchange and may track an index or another basket of
    assets. Their market price can differ from the value of the
    underlying holdings, and investors may face brokerage, spread,
    liquidity, and tracking costs. Individual shares represent exposure
    to a particular company and can be volatile. Before investing,
    understand how orders work, the role of a registered intermediary,
    account and KYC requirements, transaction costs, and the risks
    associated with the chosen security. Avoid borrowing to invest
    unless the risks are fully understood.

7.  FRAUD PREVENTION Be cautious of unsolicited stock tips,
    guaranteed-return claims, pressure to act immediately, requests to
    transfer money to personal accounts, fake trading apps,
    impersonation of regulated entities, and influencers who conceal
    conflicts of interest. Verify intermediaries and information through
    official SEBI and exchange resources. Do not share passwords, OTPs,
    or remote access to a device. Keep contract notes and account
    statements, and use official complaint channels if something appears
    wrong. A legitimate-looking website or social-media profile is not
    proof of authorization.

8.  HOW FINPILOT SHOULD ANSWER INVESTMENT QUESTIONS Explain concepts,
    risks, and how to research a product. Ask about the goal and time
    horizon only when needed to clarify the question; do not turn a
    general educational answer into a personalized recommendation. Never
    guarantee returns or claim that an asset will rise. Cite official
    SEBI or exchange resources and identify the date checked for
    information that can change. If asked to recommend a specific
    security or allocation, explain the limits of the information and
    suggest consulting a properly registered investment adviser for
    personalized advice. The user should make decisions based on their
    own circumstances and the official documents.

DOCUMENT MAINTENANCE Use current SEBI Investor Education material and
applicable official disclosures. Review links periodically, and label
outdated content rather than silently relying on it. Third-party content
may be used as a lead for research but should not override official
primary sources.
